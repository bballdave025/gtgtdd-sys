# uGTgtdD / GTD System — Working Context Summary
**Prepared for discussion with Gerry — 11 August 2026**

## 1. What I am trying to build

I am trying to build a practical personal implementation of David Allen's
_Getting Things Done_ (GTD), adapted to the way my ADHD actually works.

I have jokingly called this **uGTgtdD** — "you get GTD" / "you get get it."
The larger goal is not to become exceptionally good at maintaining a
productivity system. The goal is to get more of my time and attention aligned
with the things I actually value, and to spend less of my life simply keeping
my head above water.

A major problem I have had with productivity systems is that building,
organizing, and improving the system can itself become a project. I can get
lost in the weeds. I therefore want this implementation to be deliberately
minimal and useful.

A metaphor I am using for this is:

> **Capture the branch. Stay on the trunk.**

If a useful idea appears while I am doing something else, I want to capture
enough of it that I will not lose it, but then return to the thing I was
actually trying to accomplish. The branch is preserved rather than pursued.

A related design principle is:

> **Lean-to, not cathedral.**

The system needs to be good enough to support doing things. It does not need
to become an elaborate intellectual object in its own right.

# 2. GTD "contexts" and why I care about them

I am using **context** primarily in David Allen's GTD sense.

A context is a place, state, person, object, or circumstance that is necessary
for or particularly conducive to performing a next action.

For example:

- Some work can be done with any phone.
- Some requires any computer.
- Some requires my work laptop.
- Some requires my home desktop/tower.
- Something I need to discuss with Anastasia becomes especially actionable
  when I am with her or talking with her.
- Something involving my parents may be worth reviewing before I go to their
  house.
- A research project may require hardware or data that exists only at home.

For the moment, **NTEC and RMMFB** are good examples of the last case. Important
parts of those projects are particularly suited to my home tower because I
have the classifier there, easy access to the 14 TB drive, and much of the
image collection either on the computer or attached storage.

The purpose of context is therefore not primarily categorization.

It answers something closer to:

> **Given the circumstances I am in right now, what work is actually
> available to me?**

That is potentially extremely useful for ADHD because it reduces the number
of possibilities I have to consider at once.

## My old context notation

My old system lived substantially in Google Keep, which meant I invented
searchable strings such as:

- `#CONTEXT@obj_phone`
- `#CONTEXT@obj_any_computer`
- `#CONTEXT@ppl_Anastasia`
- `#CONTEXT@plc_home`

I do **not** care about preserving the hashtags themselves. They were a way
of making canonical-ish GTD notation work inside Google Keep.

The `@` was intentional because `@` is conventionally associated with GTD
contexts.

I further classified contexts to make them easier to find:

- `obj` — required object/tool/device
- `plc` — place
- `ppl` — person or people
- `org` — organization/group
- `info` — information-related mode/activity
- `idea` — some special opportunity/idea contexts
- `phelp` — an old category whose exact intended meaning may need recovering

Examples of people contexts included Anastasia, Julie, my parents,
Anastasia's parents, therapist, friends, work acquaintances, etc.

This can be very practical. Before calling or visiting somebody, I can look
at that person's context and see what I have accumulated that I should do,
ask, give, discuss, or remember while that opportunity exists.

# 3. Time and energy may be especially important ADHD filters

One part of my old system that I still really like used a different notation:

`#CONTEXT~...`

Again, I do not care about preserving that literal notation. The underlying
idea matters.

I had two major dimensions:

## Time available / time required

Examples included approximately:

- seconds
- at least 5 minutes
- at least 15 minutes
- at least 30 minutes
- at least 45 minutes
- at least 60 minutes
- about 2 hours
- so long that the action probably needs to be split

The intention was usually to estimate the time needed for the **next action**,
or at least the next meaningful step within that action.

This creates a useful question:

> **I have 15 minutes. What useful things actually fit into 15 minutes?**

This is different from asking myself to choose from every project and task in
my life.

I already have a category that fits this very well:

> **chip away little by little**

For example, some Google Drive archaeology can be done during a 10–15 minute
bus ride. I also have a long-term project involving digitized family photos:
I want eventually to identify them, add the equivalent of information written
on the backs, and connect them with a journal/history system. That is another
thing that can be advanced in small pieces without becoming the day's main
project.

These are useful tasks for otherwise awkward fragments of time.

## Energy required

My old scale was:

- barely alive
- 1
- 2
- 3
- ...
- 9
- up to eleven

The exact psychometrics obviously do not matter. The point is to represent
the amount/type of energy required.

For example:

**Eating breakfast** can be done when I am barely alive.

**Scanning incoming paper/mail** should be low-energy work, perhaps roughly
1–3. This is something I want to begin doing regularly so that paper does not
accumulate into stacks and useful information becomes digital.

**Hard-core RMMFB implementation or serious research writing** might require
something like 8–11.

This gives me another extremely useful question:

> **Given the energy I actually have, what can I successfully do?**

Instead of deciding that I "should work on RMMFB" and then failing because I
do not currently have the cognitive resources for difficult implementation,
the system could show me meaningful work that fits my present state.

I would love to say that time and energy have already been my most useful
filters. They have **not**, because I have never implemented them consistently
enough.

But I think the underlying idea is very promising.

That is part of what I am trying to fix now.

# 4. Priority and urgency are the big missing piece

This is the part I especially want to discuss in therapy.

I understand the **definitions** of priority and urgency intellectually.

I do not think I understand how to use them reliably in my actual life.

My old system had:

### Priority

- priority 1
- priority 2
- priority 3
- priority 4

### Urgency

- urgency 1
- urgency 2
- urgency 3
- urgency 4
- urgency 5

I do not currently remember why priority had four levels and urgency had five.
That may not matter. I do not want to preserve an arbitrary old scale merely
because I once created it.

The much more important problem is:

> **How do I decide what priority and urgency actually mean operationally?**

ADHD seems particularly relevant here.

Something being genuinely important does not necessarily make it salient
enough for me to initiate it.

Conversely, something that suddenly feels urgent can capture an enormous
amount of attention even if its objective importance does not justify that.

There are therefore distinctions I want to get better at making:

> **Important**
>
> versus
>
> **feels important right now**

and

> **Urgent**
>
> versus
>
> **my brain is treating this as urgent**

I do not need another abstract explanation of the words "important" and
"urgent." I understand those.

I need a practical method for looking at real things in my life and deciding:

1. How important is this?
2. How urgent is this?
3. What evidence am I using?
4. Is my emotional/salience system giving me useful information?
5. Is it distorting the decision?
6. What should I actually do next as a result?

I expect this to be one of the places where therapy and the external GTD
system can complement each other.

The system can externalize decisions, but I need to develop better judgment
about what values to put into it.

# 5. Other pieces of the old system

Some frequently used GTD concepts had deliberately short tags because I did
not want to type or search for something like `#CONTEXT@...` constantly.

Examples included things like:

- inbox
- next action
- tickler file
- waiting for

Some used an underscore so that they would sort toward the beginning of
lists:

- `_inbox`
- `_next_action`

That punctuation was therefore often a **usability hack**, not a deep
conceptual distinction.

This is important as I move away from Google Keep. I do not want to reproduce
old syntax merely because it exists.

I want to preserve the **behavior that made the syntax useful**:

- very fast capture
- easy searching
- useful sorting
- low typing burden
- low decision burden
- easy retrieval when a context becomes available

I am currently considering Asana as the primary GTD/action system, while
Google Keep can remain useful for quick temporary capture, partial prompts,
links, and notes.

Google Drive is becoming the durable **General Reference** system.

The broad division I am moving toward is therefore something like:

**Capture**
→ get the thing safely out of working memory

**Clarify**
→ what is this, and is anything actionable?

**Projects / Next Actions**
→ actionable material lives in the GTD/task system

**General Reference**
→ durable non-actionable information lives in Google Drive

The important point is that capture should not require me to fully classify
something immediately.

# 6. Minimal project creation

I also found an old checklist for creating projects.

It was deliberately minimal.

The rough process included:

1. Create a project title.
2. Put it on the project list.
3. Add a short description if needed.
4. Identify the next action.
5. Give a first-pass description of what "done" looks like.
6. Add useful contexts.
7. Add color/collaborators if relevant.

One instruction I particularly like allowed the "done looks like" description
to be imperfect and tagged as needing work.

That is important because otherwise defining the project can itself become
an obstacle to starting it.

The desired behavior is:

> **Define it well enough to move. Improve the definition later if necessary.**

I do not want every new project to require completing a large metadata form.
If project creation itself has a high activation cost, I will avoid creating
projects, leave things in inboxes, or hold them in my head.

# 7. What success would look like

I do **not** want a system that makes me perfectly organized.

I want a system that helps me answer questions such as:

> I have 15 minutes on this bus. What could I advance?

> I'm exhausted. What worthwhile thing can I still do?

> I unexpectedly have two hours and good cognitive energy. What important
> work should get that scarce resource?

> I'm about to talk with Anastasia. What have I been waiting to discuss with
> her?

> I'm going to my parents' house. Is there anything I can only/easily do
> there?

> I'm at the home tower with the big drive connected and I have high energy.
> Which research next action becomes available?

> This feels incredibly urgent. **Is it?**

> This is genuinely important but has no deadline. How do I keep it from
> disappearing for six months?

That last pair is probably particularly important for me.

My research/portfolio work illustrates the problem. I have projects that
matter enormously to my longer-term goals but that do not necessarily produce
an external crisis if I neglect them today. Meanwhile, ordinary life
continually generates things that *do* produce immediate signals.

Without a trustworthy external system, the immediate signals tend to win.

# 8. What I would especially like Gerry's help thinking about

The technical implementation in Asana is something I can work out separately.

The therapeutic/executive-function question I want help with is more basic:

> **How can I get better at deciding what deserves my attention when my
> subjective sense of salience, importance, and urgency may not correspond
> cleanly to what I actually value or what objectively needs action?**

In particular:

- How should I distinguish importance from emotional salience?
- How should I distinguish real urgency from felt urgency?
- How much should I trust my immediate sense that something "needs doing
  now"?
- What kinds of external rules could help without becoming rigid?
- How can I recognize when prioritization itself has turned into avoidance?
- How can I deliberately protect important, non-urgent work?
- How can I choose something appropriate for my actual energy instead of
  treating low energy as either failure or permission to do nothing?
- How can I use a system to reduce decisions without letting maintaining the
  system become another source of decisions?

The goal is ultimately not better task metadata.

It is to make it easier for me to **do the things I have decided matter**,
including family, work, health, learning, research, and longer-term career
development, while spending less cognitive energy repeatedly deciding what
I am supposed to be doing.

That is the piece of GTD I am hoping to make genuinely work with my ADHD,
rather than merely understanding it intellectually.
